The United States is simultaneously absorbing casualties from Iranian strikes in Jordan, watching its brief diplomatic opening with Beijing close over newly declassified election interference documents, and managing the most significant NATO commitment to Ukraine since the war began. These are not separate crises. They share causes, reinforce each other, and are converging on a set of decision points that will define U.S. strategic positioning for the remainder of 2026.

None of this was inevitable two months ago. A May summit in Beijing between President Trump and President Xi appeared to put trade normalization and Taiwan de-escalation back on the table. That opening has now narrowed sharply. Simultaneously, the U.S.-Iran conflict that began in late February has entered a deadlier phase, with American service members killed on Jordanian soil. Europe, watching both theaters, has consolidated its own posture with a new anti-ballistic missile coalition and record military commitments to Kyiv.

## What We Know

**Iran-Hormuz theater.** The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to normal commercial shipping since February 28, when U.S. and Israeli forces launched airstrikes against Iranian military infrastructure. The IRGC's response has been methodical: on July 11, the IRGC Navy announced closure of the strait and struck a Cyprus-flagged tanker. By July 13, two UAE tankers had been hit by Iranian missiles in the southern strait. The Guardian reported on July 17 that U.S. CENTCOM had executed its seventh consecutive night of strikes against "Iranian military command centers, air defense and coastal surveillance sites, maritime capabilities, missile and drone launch sites, and communications networks."

The human cost escalated sharply over the weekend. CENTCOM confirmed that two U.S. service members were killed and a third is missing following Iranian strikes on a position in Jordan. As of this writing, a fourth casualty has been reported by Newsweek. The Fox News live update page cited CENTCOM's characterization of the environment as offering "the potential for unforeseen escalation" — a phrase that functions simultaneously as warning and admission that the conflict's trajectory is not fully under U.S. control.

Iran used a Chinese-supplied reconnaissance satellite, purchased in 2024 and reported by the Financial Times in April 2026, to target U.S. military assets across the region. The Washington Post separately corroborated this reporting, per the Institute for the Study of War's July 18 special report. The ISW also assessed that the initial U.S.-Iran ceasefire negotiated earlier this year has functionally ended.

**US-China: election interference claims reopen the wound.** On July 16, President Trump delivered a primetime address in which he alleged that China had stolen voter data on 220 million Americans during the 2020 election cycle. The White House released four batches of declassified intelligence documents to support the claim. China's government called the accusations "groundless." Beijing's reaction, reported by the Washington Times, was sharp enough to register as a formal diplomatic protest.

Foreign Policy assessed on July 17 that the bilateral relationship is "more volatile than ever," noting that despite the apparent sincerity of both Trump and Xi at their May summit, structural fault lines — Taiwan, technology controls, and now election interference allegations — have reasserted themselves faster than either side's diplomatic machinery can manage.

Meanwhile, Mondaq's US-China Trade Monthly for July details the operational consequences: China's new counter-extraterritoriality regulations and the Trump administration's enhanced customs enforcement strategy now directly conflict in ways that force multinationals to simultaneously comply with incompatible legal obligations from two governments.

**Indo-Pacific: SLBM test, PLA naval posture, and Sino-Russian exercises.** On July 6, the PLA conducted a rare test of a submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM). PRC state media confirmed an unspecified ballistic missile nuclear submarine (SSBN) fired the missile; Maritime Safety Administration notices in both the Bohai Sea and South China Sea suggest the launch location remains officially ambiguous, though the test's purpose — demonstrating credible nuclear second-strike capability — is not. The ISW's China-Taiwan update from July 17 also documented what Taiwanese authorities assess as a Chinese-directed transnational repression operation: a Hong Kong national arrested after allegedly assaulting the CEO of a Taiwan-based think tank focused on cross-strait relations.

Simultaneously, China and Russia wrapped up their "Joint Sea-2026" naval exercise, launched July 6 near the port of Qingdao. The exercise involved surface, submarine, aerial, and support units from both navies — a demonstration of the Sino-Russian partnership's growing operational integration.

**European security: NATO Ankara summit and a new ballistic missile coalition.** On July 13, on the sidelines of the NATO summit in Ankara, France, Denmark, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom, and Ukraine signed a declaration establishing what the DW and NATO.int reporting describes as an "Anti-Ballistic Coalition." NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte attended a parallel Coalition of the Willing session in Paris, where allies and Canada committed to €70 billion in military equipment, assistance, and training for Ukraine in 2026, with at least the same pledged for 2027. Rutte specifically cited recent Russian missile attacks on Kyiv in urging continued donations.

## What's Driving It

The Iran escalation is self-sustaining in ways that earlier confrontations with Tehran were not. The February strikes destroyed significant portions of Iran's air defense and coastal surveillance architecture. That degradation removed some of Iran's deterrent capacity. The IRGC response — mining, tanker strikes, attacks on U.S. positions in Jordan — reflects a force using asymmetric tools precisely because its conventional deterrent has been reduced. Each exchange narrows the range of outcomes that don't involve direct Iranian strikes on U.S. personnel.

China's economic coercion tools — counter-extraterritoriality regulations, export controls on rare earths and specialized materials — are being deployed with greater precision than in previous cycles. The election interference declassification is partly domestic politics (Trump has clear incentives to position China as a midterm threat narrative), but the underlying intelligence reporting, if accurate, represents a qualitative escalation from influence operations to data infrastructure compromise at scale. Beijing's furious public denial suggests it did not anticipate the declassification's timing.

The Sino-Russian military convergence is no longer theoretical. Joint naval exercises in a geographically sensitive zone, combined with China's supply of reconnaissance satellite capability to Iran, describe a network of relationships with concrete operational consequences for U.S. force protection across multiple theaters simultaneously.

NATO's €70 billion commitment reflects European recognition that American strategic attention is now divided among at least three concurrent crises. The Anti-Ballistic Coalition is a direct response to the pattern of Russian ballistic and cruise missile strikes on Ukrainian cities — and, inferentially, to the recognition that Europe's own cities may not be permanently immune.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security.** The convergence of Iranian, Chinese, and Russian pressure on U.S. forces and interests across different theaters simultaneously is not accidental. CENTCOM is absorbing casualties in Jordan while managing Hormuz interdiction, the Indo-Pacific Command is tracking SLBM tests and PLA naval exercises, and the intelligence community is processing the satellite-targeting capability Iran acquired from China. Force allocation decisions made in one theater now have readiness implications in others.

The election interference declassifications add a domestic political dimension that complicates diplomacy. Whatever the documents' merit, the public accusation of 220 million compromised voter records — made in primetime — constrains the administration's ability to resume the trade normalization track from May without political cost.

**For businesses.** Multinationals operating in both the U.S. and China now face directly conflicting legal obligations, as Mondaq documents in detail. Hormuz disruption has been ongoing since February; any company relying on Persian Gulf shipping for energy or logistics inputs that has not yet restructured its supply chain is operating on borrowed time. Oil markets have been affected since late February; this week's tanker strikes represent a potential step toward more sustained disruption.

**For allies.** The NATO Anti-Ballistic Coalition signals that Europe's frontline states are no longer willing to treat ballistic missile defense as a theoretical priority. The inclusion of Ukraine in the coalition — not merely as a recipient but as a signatory — is a political statement as much as a military one. For Gulf partners, the Iranian tanker strikes on UAE-flagged vessels represent a direct threat; how Abu Dhabi and Riyadh respond to that pressure will shape regional alignment for years.

## What to Watch

**Iranian casualty threshold.** Each U.S. service member killed in Jordan raises the domestic political cost of the current graduated-response approach. A threshold somewhere between three and six U.S. combat deaths likely triggers a qualitative escalation in U.S. strike targets — potentially including assets inside Iran proper that have so far been off the target list.

**China's response to declassification.** Beijing has options beyond diplomatic protest: it could restrict rare earth exports, accelerate targeted economic coercion, or increase PLA activity near Taiwan in the next 10-14 days. Watch for any formal suspension of working-level diplomatic channels established at the May summit.

**Hormuz shipping insurance and energy prices.** Lloyd's of London and regional war-risk insurers are the leading indicator here. A sustained premium spike above the levels seen after the July 13 tanker strikes would signal that markets have revised their baseline assumption from "temporary disruption" to "structural closure."

**Ukraine front-line developments.** Russia's rejection of a local ceasefire for return of soldiers' bodies (Reuters, July 4) and its continued denial of Ukrainian advances suggest the eastern front remains fluid. The NATO €70 billion commitment guarantees Ukrainian resupply through 2026, but battlefield trajectory over the next 30 days will determine whether that funding is buying stabilization or extended attrition.

**PLA second-strike posture.** The July 6 SLBM test was a signal, not an anomaly. Watch for additional PRC maritime exclusion notices and any further SSBN deployments, which the Pentagon tracks as indicators of escalating nuclear readiness posture.
