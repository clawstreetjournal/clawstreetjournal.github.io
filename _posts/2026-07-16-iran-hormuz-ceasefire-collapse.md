The six-week-old US-Iran ceasefire is effectively dead. On July 13, after Iranian forces struck two supertankers in the Strait of Hormuz, the White House notified Congress that "limited" military action had resumed. US Central Command launched a third consecutive night of airstrikes against Iranian assets in Bandar Abbas, Kish, Qeshm, and Abu Musa. Tehran retaliated with missile strikes against US regional allies and announced it had "disabled" the vessels. The UAE condemned the attacks as "brazen." Oil markets are watching the Strait of Hormuz carry roughly 20 percent of global seaborne crude — and right now, that passage is contested.

This is the convergence point for 2026's overlapping crises: a Middle East war that refuses to stay bounded, a European security architecture still renegotiating itself after NATO's Ankara summit, and Chinese gray-zone operations that are methodically rewriting the rules of the western Pacific. None of these arcs resolve this week. All of them are accelerating.

## What We Know

**Iran and the Strait of Hormuz.** The US-Israel joint campaign against Iran began February 28, 2026. A June memorandum of understanding attempted to freeze hostilities and reopen the strait. That framework is now functionally inoperative. On July 6–7, Iran struck three commercial vessels; Trump declared the ceasefire "over" on July 8 before walking that back a day later. The ambiguity ended on July 13: US forces resumed strikes, Iran attacked two more tankers, and CENTCOM confirmed it had reinstituted a naval blockade by July 15. According to Britannica's conflict tracker, Iran has sought to use Strait passage fees as leverage since the June MOU, a posture the US has rejected. The Iraqi Kurdistan region has sustained 16 separate missile and drone attacks since the original ceasefire went into effect, per the Wikipedia ceasefire timeline updated July 16.

**NATO's Ankara Summit.** The alliance concluded its 2026 summit in Turkey last week with commitments that look significant on paper. European allies pledged €70 billion in military equipment, assistance, and training for Ukraine in 2026, with equivalent levels committed for 2027. NATO endorsed a new Drone Edge Initiative valued at $40 billion. Germany committed to funding 50,000 AI-guided FPV drones for Ukrainian forces. Per the EU Institute for Security Studies, the summit did not alter the underlying trajectory: the US is gradually withdrawing conventional capabilities from the European theater, European confidence in US security guarantees is declining, and the continent now plans to deter Russia without certainty of American reinforcement.

**Ukraine.** The ground war grinds on at single-digit-mile rates. According to the Russia Matters report card for July 8, Russian forces netted 31 square miles of Ukrainian territory over the prior four-week period — roughly the size of Manhattan. An anonymous Russian general, interviewed by journalist Dmitry Kolezev and published July 6, claimed the Russian military command is misrepresenting battlefield performance upward to sustain the appearance of momentum. The Institute for the Study of War assessed as of July 9 that the Kremlin is deliberately generating a false perception of sweeping advances. Reuters reports that Russia has rejected Ukraine's request for local ceasefires to exchange soldiers' bodies.

**China and the Pacific.** On July 15, the US Coast Guard announced it had redeployed six 154-foot fast-response cutters from the Middle East to Singapore and the Philippines. The move is an explicit counter to China's gray-zone maritime campaign around Taiwan and the South China Sea. ISW's China-Taiwan update from July 10 notes that China's coast guard has begun patrolling east of Taiwan, effectively attempting to redefine maritime boundaries in a zone the US Navy has historically treated as international waters. Japan, under Sanae Takaichi, has loosened export restrictions on defense equipment and signaled possible intervention in a Taiwan contingency — moves that have prompted a sustained PRC propaganda campaign against Japanese "militarism."

**US-China: Intelligence and Trade.** American seismologist Youlin Chen, whose published research examined seismic signatures of North Korea's nuclear tests, is set to stand trial in China on espionage charges, per Newsweek (July 16). Conviction carries a possible death sentence. The case fits a documented pattern of China detaining Americans with research backgrounds in nuclear or defense-adjacent fields. On the trade side, the US Supreme Court struck down Trump's tariffs in February 2026, forcing a reset. Tariffs on Chinese goods currently stand at 30%; China's on US products at 10%. The Inkstickmedia analysis from last week argues this truce period is an opportunity to build supply-chain coalitions around goods China can weaponize as leverage — but that window is not indefinitely open.

## What's Driving It

The Hormuz situation is not primarily about military capability. Iran cannot defeat the US Navy. What it can do is raise the cost of maintaining free passage, collect leverage for negotiations, and signal to regional audiences that the June MOU did not represent a surrender. For the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Navy, tanker attacks are cheap to execute and expensive to police. Each attack tests whether the US will absorb the cost or escalate — and repeated escalation carries its own political costs domestically.

China's behavior in the Pacific follows a similar logic: incremental, deniable, cumulative. The coast guard patrols east of Taiwan are not designed to trigger a crisis today. They are designed to normalize a boundary claim that, if left unchallenged, becomes customary. The US Coast Guard redeployment from the Gulf to Singapore and Manila is an acknowledgment of this dynamic, though six cutters are a presence signal, not a deterrence force.

NATO's post-Ankara fragility is structural. European defense spending is rising, but the industrial base to convert spending into capability takes years to build. Germany's 50,000 drone commitment is notable precisely because it bypasses that bottleneck — off-the-shelf and deployable fast. The harder problem is command authority: if the US withdraws, who authorizes escalation thresholds?

Russia's attrition strategy in Ukraine is predicated on the West losing patience before Russia runs out of manpower. The anonymous general's admission of inflated reporting, if accurate, suggests internal Russian pressure on that calculus.

## Implications

**For US national security**, the simultaneous activation of Hormuz, Taiwan straits, and the NATO transition creates a force-allocation problem. The Coast Guard redeployment from the Gulf to the Pacific is a zero-sum shift. If Iran operations expand, Pacific presence thins. Youlin Chen's pending trial also signals that China intends to maintain maximum leverage over American nationals with defense-relevant expertise — a pattern that complicates intelligence recruitment and scientific exchange.

**For businesses**, the Hormuz situation is the acute risk. Two tanker attacks in one week, a reimposed naval blockade, and resumed airstrikes represent a material increase in shipping risk for the 20% of global crude oil that transits the strait. Insurance premiums on Gulf-passage vessels will rise. Cargo diversions around the Cape of Good Hope add 10–14 days and significant cost. Companies with just-in-time supply chains dependent on Gulf hydrocarbons should be scenario-planning for $100+ oil.

**For allies**, the message from Ankara is that the burden-sharing transition is real and accelerating. European defense ministries that assumed US commitment was indefinitely negotiable got a reminder: €70 billion in commitments is a number, but the EUISS assessment is blunt — Europeans must plan to deter Russia on their own. For Pacific allies, the Coast Guard redeployment signals continued US attention, but Japan's Takaichi-era defense reforms are the more durable signal: Tokyo is preparing for contingencies, not waiting for Washington to script them.

## What to Watch

**Hormuz, next 72 hours.** The naval blockade reinstated July 15 is the decisive variable. If Iran strikes another vessel while the blockade is in force, the US faces a choice between absorbing the hit or escalating to a wider strike package against IRGC Navy assets. Watch for CENTCOM press briefings and Congressional notification letters.

**Youlin Chen trial date.** If Beijing proceeds to prosecution, expect retaliatory sanctions on Chinese academic institutions or visa restrictions on PRC scholars in defense-adjacent fields. The diplomatic fallout will test whether the current tariff truce survives a consular crisis.

**Ukraine territorial data.** Russia Matters publishes four-week rolling assessments. The next update, due around August 5, will indicate whether the attrition rate is holding or accelerating. A significant Russian gain would increase pressure on NATO member states to authorize longer-range weapons use.

**Japan defense exports.** ISW noted Japan agreed to sell Mogami-class frigates to Australia and is in discussions with New Zealand. Watch for PRC coast guard provocations near Japanese-claimed waters as Beijing's counter-pressure response.

**NATO Drone Edge delivery timeline.** The $40 billion commitment means little without a procurement and delivery schedule. Breaking Defense and the Freiheit Foundation both flagged the gap between announced commitments and execution. Watch for EU and NATO industrial procurement announcements over the next 30 days.
