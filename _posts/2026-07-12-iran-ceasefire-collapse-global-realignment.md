The agreement that was supposed to end the 2026 Iran war is falling apart in real time. On July 8, U.S. forces carried out fresh strikes on Iranian targets after what the Trump administration declared was a violation of the interim ceasefire arrangement. Iran's foreign ministry responded by declaring the memorandum of understanding "ineffective." By July 9, CNN was reporting that the ceasefire had crumbled entirely, and oil prices were climbing on fears of a prolonged Strait of Hormuz disruption.

This is not simply a Middle East story. The same week that U.S.-Iranian tensions reignited, Washington convened NATO allies in Ankara for a summit that exposed deep structural fractures in the alliance. Simultaneously, Trump held a nearly 90-minute call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, while diplomatic signals toward Beijing suggested a September summit is still on track. What looks like simultaneous chaos is better understood as the United States managing multiple high-stakes dealmaking tracks at once — with compressing timelines on all of them.

The common thread: Washington is trying to close deals before domestic political gravity pulls each track apart, and adversaries know it.

## What We Know

**Iran and the Strait.** The original conflict began February 28, 2026, when U.S. and Israeli forces launched coordinated strikes on Iranian nuclear and military infrastructure. A ceasefire and memorandum of understanding followed weeks later, but the arrangement was fragile from the start. On July 8, the U.S. carried out another round of strikes after Trump announced the truce was over. Iran's Foreign Ministry called the accord "ineffective" following continued U.S. and Israeli attacks. Radio Free Europe/Liberty reported that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu confirmed via his office that he and Trump spoke by phone on July 9, with Trump updating Netanyahu on "American moves in the Gulf." Netanyahu acknowledged the conflict "was not yet over" while noting Iran had been weakened by two previous military campaigns. The Hill reported oil prices rising on the renewed uncertainty, with market analysts pointing to Strait of Hormuz transit risk as the primary driver.

**Russia-Ukraine.** The Institute for the Study of War's July 9 assessment revealed an anonymous Russian general — speaking to journalist Dmitry Kolezev — who alleged that Russian military command is falsifying battlefield reports to conceal a war of attrition Russia cannot sustain. ISW assessed this as consistent with prior reporting on command pressure to maintain the appearance of momentum. On July 6, Trump spoke with Putin for nearly 90 minutes, with Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov describing the call as "business-like" and stating Trump "reaffirmed his readiness to facilitate an early termination of the hostilities." Ukraine's President Volodymyr Zelensky called on NATO allies for additional support following Russia's latest strikes on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure, which Time reported on July 6.

**US-China.** China Briefing confirmed this week that following Trump's May visit to Beijing, China agreed to purchase at least $17 billion in U.S. agricultural products annually through 2028 — in addition to existing soybean commitments. On July 4, Xi Jinping sent a congratulatory Independence Day message to Trump, which South China Morning Post described as a departure from convention. Bloomberg reported July 7 that Xi had agreed to release an imprisoned Christian pastor at Trump's request, a move framed as a confidence-building gesture ahead of a September bilateral summit.

**NATO Ankara Summit.** The alliance held its annual summit in Ankara, Turkey on July 7–8, producing what NATO published as the Ankara Summit Declaration. Forbes analyst Mark Temnycky assessed the summit as revealing a coalition still divided on burden-sharing, Ukraine's long-term security guarantees, and the scope of member commitments. The Kyiv Independent described the declaration as heavy on language and light on binding commitments. Time's assessment, published July 7, was blunter: "Stop Mourning the Old NATO. Build the New One" — arguing that the alliance's relevance increasingly depends on European defense autonomy rather than U.S. reassurance.

**Indo-Pacific.** ISW's China-Taiwan Update from July 10 tracked continued People's Liberation Army activity near Taiwan. A separate report from Defence Security Asia noted a China-Russia joint naval exercise in the Pacific in late June that U.S. analysts viewed as a signal about maritime coordination between the two powers. No new crisis-level events were reported in the South China Sea this week, though the broader context of U.S. attention diverted to Iran is not lost on regional observers.

## What's Driving It

The Iran situation is driven by three overlapping dynamics. First, the original ceasefire was negotiated under domestic and electoral pressure, without a durable enforcement mechanism. Second, Iran's strategic calculus has shifted: the IRGC's conventional capabilities have been degraded, but Tehran still retains asymmetric leverage over the Strait of Hormuz, which carries roughly 20–21% of global oil supply. Third, the Trump administration faces a credibility problem. Walking away from the MOU without a successor agreement sends a signal — to allies and adversaries alike — that U.S. commitments have a short shelf life.

On Russia, the reported falsification of battlefield reports is analytically significant. If accurate, it suggests Putin is managing an internal information environment as much as a military campaign. The 90-minute Trump-Putin call, framed in Moscow as productive, is more precisely described as a holding pattern. Russia has no incentive to conclude peace on terms favorable to Ukraine; the current attritional approach suits Moscow so long as Western resolve remains uncertain.

The US-China agricultural deal and the Xi-Trump prisoner release signal a managed détente — neither full normalization nor open competition. Both sides are protecting economic interdependencies while preserving the ability to compete on technology, Taiwan, and maritime access. The September summit is the next hard deadline. Beijing is calibrating goodwill gestures to smooth the path without making structural concessions.

The NATO summit's Ankara setting was itself a statement: Turkey, long an awkward alliance member, hosted what became a forum for European powers to assert greater autonomy. That dynamic — European defense architecture evolving faster than U.S. strategic engagement can track — is likely to accelerate, particularly if U.S. focus remains dominated by Iran and domestic politics.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security.** The renewed Iran conflict creates a third active theater alongside Ukraine and the Indo-Pacific. The U.S. military is not overstretched in a 1990s sense — precision-strike capacity and ISR assets remain formidable — but escalatory management becomes harder as more crises run simultaneously. If Iran attempts even a partial Strait of Hormuz closure, the military response calculus changes significantly. Combined Chiefs and CENTCOM planning must now account for a scenario where Iran chooses degraded-but-symbolic access interference rather than full closure.

**For energy markets and business.** Oil prices have already moved on Hormuz risk. Corporate treasury teams with exposure to energy-linked supply chains should treat current volatility as the floor, not the ceiling. Brent crude futures-implied scenarios price in a premium for continued uncertainty. Separately, the US-China agricultural commitment provides a near-term floor for U.S. agribusiness, but the deal's durability depends on the September summit proceeding without incident. Any Taiwan-adjacent provocation between now and September could collapse the trade track.

**For allies.** NATO members attending Ankara left with a declaration but not a doctrine. The gap between rhetoric on Ukraine and material commitment remains. European defense industry capacity is scaling, but on a 3–5 year timeline. In the near term, allies face a choice between deeper autonomy investments and continued dependence on U.S. guarantees that are increasingly conditioned on political alignment with Washington's transactional approach. That is a harder choice for Warsaw and the Baltics than it is for Paris.

## What to Watch

**Iran next 72 hours.** The critical indicator is whether Iran takes any action in the Strait — even demonstrative, such as increased naval presence or harassment of commercial shipping. Any interference with tanker traffic will trigger immediate market moves and likely a U.S. military response. Trump's stated willingness to "hit Iran hard again tonight" (per The Guardian, July 7) suggests a low threshold for escalation.

**Russia.** Watch for any change in Russian offensive tempo around Kostiantynivka, which Ukraine's Zelensky denied had fallen as of July 4. If Russia publicly claims the city and Moscow's internal reporting confirms it, that will test whether the anonymous general's account of falsified reporting reflects a broader command failure or an isolated incident.

**US-China September summit.** The prisoner release and agricultural commitment are confidence signals. The test is whether both sides can keep the Taiwan and technology competition tracks from intersecting the economic track before September. Any PLA military activity around Taiwan beyond routine patrols would change the calculus immediately.

**NATO's internal divisions.** The Kyiv Independent's assessment that the Ankara Declaration is light on binding commitments should be treated as a working hypothesis, not a verdict. Watch which member states follow up Ankara with actual procurement announcements or bilateral security commitments to Ukraine in the next 30 days.

**Oil price floor.** West Texas Intermediate above $90 sustained for more than two weeks would signal that markets have priced in a new baseline risk premium on Middle East supply routes — with downstream effects on inflation expectations and central bank posture in the U.S. and EU.
