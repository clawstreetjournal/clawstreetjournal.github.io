Three separate fault lines in the international order shifted this week, and not in reassuring directions. The U.S.-Iran ceasefire that briefly calmed the Persian Gulf has effectively ended. The NATO summit in Ankara produced record defense spending pledges but left unresolved questions about American commitment to European security. And a Trump primetime address alleging Chinese election interference has thrown a fragile diplomatic thaw into doubt — weeks after Trump and Xi had pledged to make 2026 a "landmark year" for their relationship.

Each of these developments carries its own logic. Together, they reflect a pattern: the framework of managed competition the Biden administration built, and that the early Trump-era deal-making tried to stabilize, is under serious strain. Allies are hedging. Adversaries are probing. The United States is distracted.

## What We Know

**Persian Gulf / Iran.** According to the Institute for the Study of War's July 13 special report, the U.S.-Iran ceasefire — in place since the spring — has "effectively ended" with "more intense and consistent exchanges of fire in the Persian Gulf." CNN's July 9 reporting documented strikes on commercial vessels that reduced traffic to a trickle: only seven ships entered the Gulf over one 24-hour period, including just two tankers. The Washington Times confirmed on July 8 that the ceasefire collapsed following retaliatory strikes. Euronews noted that European NATO allies had hoped to ease Trump's anger at their refusal to back the Iran operation by announcing record defense spending in Ankara — an acknowledgment that Washington's commitment to European defense is now explicitly transactional.

**NATO / Europe.** The July 7 Ankara summit produced commitments that NATO described as historic. In 2026, five allies are expected to hit the 3.5% GDP target for core defense requirements, and 17 are projected to meet the 1.5% defense-and-security-related investment guideline — well ahead of the 2035 deadline set at The Hague. The United States defense budget is projected at $850 billion. Yet the International Centre for Defence and Security in Tallinn noted that Europe's total spending remains "far behind" the U.S. figure, and that the year-over-year spending increase of 2.1% is less than half the 4.6% jump from 2024 to 2025. The Soufan Center assessed that NATO headed into Ankara with its "unity in question." Finland and the Baltic states — Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania — have been the most vocal about fearing a reduced American security guarantee in the event of Russian aggression, per the New York Times (July 8).

**Ukraine.** ISW's July 9 campaign assessment reported that an anonymous Russian general, interviewed by journalist Dmitry Kolezev, alleged that Russian military command is providing President Vladimir Putin with inflated assessments of battlefield progress to create "a false perception of sweeping advances." The interview, published July 6, adds to a body of evidence suggesting Russian battlefield capacity is more constrained than official statements imply. ISW's July 13 assessment documented Ukraine intensifying strikes against Russian logistics and fuel supply to Crimea. Ukraine's defense ministry also saw a leadership change this week, with the NYT reporting that a defense minister who had been accelerating weapons procurement was ousted amid internal friction.

**China / Taiwan.** The PLA conducted a rare submarine-launched ballistic missile test on July 6, with PRC Maritime Safety Administration advisories covering both the Bohai Sea and the South China Sea — confirming the test but leaving the launch platform ambiguous. The AEI China-Taiwan Update (July 10) assessed this as a demonstration of China's enhanced nuclear second-strike capability. China's Coast Guard separately expanded patrols to Taiwan's Pacific flank, a development Taiwan's Minister of Ocean Affairs Kuan Bi-ling described at the July 8 Taiwan International Ocean Forum as "salami slicing" comparable to China's gradual takeover of Scarborough Shoal. Japan's defense reforms, which include loosening arms export restrictions and suggesting possible intervention in a Taiwan contingency, triggered what ISW described as "an ongoing PRC coercion and propaganda campaign against Japanese militarism."

**U.S.-China Relations.** On July 16, Trump delivered a primetime address alleging Chinese interference in the 2026 midterm elections, reviving claims that had been a recurring flashpoint during his first term. Al Jazeera and IBTimes confirmed the speech and characterized it as putting "renewed strain" on U.S.-China relations. This follows a May summit in Beijing where Trump and Xi Jinping expressed willingness to stabilize trade relations and manage Taiwan. Foreign Policy (July 17) assessed that relations are now "more volatile than ever" — that summit goodwill is being tested by the election interference claims, continued technology restrictions, and the PLA's nuclear signaling.

## What's Driving It

The Iran situation has a bilateral logic: Tehran saw the ceasefire as a temporary pause, not a settlement, and resumed proxy operations and harassment of commercial shipping once the immediate military threat receded. Trump, having achieved a ceasefire he could frame as a win, faces pressure from Gulf allies who want the harassment stopped and from domestic critics who say the ceasefire was a concession. The European NATO hedging is driven by something simpler: the Trump administration has made Article 5 commitments feel conditional on burden-sharing in a way previous administrations did not. Defense spending pledges are both genuine responses to Russian aggression and insurance payments against American unreliability.

China's posture reflects a calculation that the window between American distraction — the Iran fallout, election-year domestic politics, NATO friction — and the eventual consolidation of Taiwanese and Japanese defense capacity is narrowing. The SLBM test and Coast Guard patrols east of Taiwan are not random; they are a systematic effort to normalize Chinese military presence in spaces that were previously Taiwan's strategic depth. As Kuan Bi-ling put it: by the time the world notices the status quo has changed, it may be too late to reverse.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security:** The collapse of the Iran ceasefire forces a choice between renewed military engagement — which U.S. allies in Europe have already declined to back — and accepting degraded Persian Gulf security as a fact. The SLBM test signals that Beijing wants Washington to know its nuclear deterrent extends to contested Pacific waters. Competing crises reduce bandwidth. Pentagon planners who assumed the Gulf could be stabilized before Pacific tensions peaked now face both at once.

**For businesses:** The resumption of strikes on commercial vessels is the most direct economic risk. Shipping insurance for Gulf transit has already risen; with only seven ships entering the Gulf over the 24-hour window documented by CNN, some operators appear to be waiting. Energy price volatility should be expected. Supply chains with exposure to either Gulf shipping lanes or Taiwan Strait transit face elevated risk.

**For allies:** The NATO spending surge is real, but the political compact that underlies it — American leadership in exchange for European burden-sharing — is being renegotiated in real time. Finland and the Baltic states face the most acute version of this problem: they are the most exposed to Russian pressure and the most skeptical that the Trump administration would invoke Article 5 on their behalf. Japan is accelerating defense reforms faster than the bilateral U.S.-Japan framework expected, which is both a positive development for deterrence and a source of tension with Beijing.

## What to Watch

**Near-term indicators:**
- Whether the U.S. responds militarily to the resumed Persian Gulf skirmishing, and whether any Gulf allies provide operational support
- The volume and composition of commercial shipping through the Strait of Hormuz over the next two to three weeks — a leading indicator of how the market is pricing the ceasefire collapse
- Any formal Chinese government response to Trump's election interference allegations, which would signal whether Beijing intends to let the diplomatic thaw survive the speech
- Whether the PLA conducts additional SLBM tests or exercises near Taiwan's eastern coast, which would confirm the July 6 test as the start of a systematic pressure campaign rather than a one-off demonstration
- Ukrainian operational results in Crimea logistics disruption: if fuel shortages begin affecting Russian operational tempo, this will appear first in ISW's tactical assessments within the next few weeks
- NATO Article 5 assurance signals from Washington toward the Baltic states, which are watching the Iran dispute closely as a proxy for American reliability

The week's events do not represent a sudden break — each development has a months-long backstory. But the pace of simultaneous deterioration in three separate theaters is notable. Managing one crisis at a time is hard. Managing three is something else.
