Three crises moved in parallel this week. Iran is negotiating the terms of its own ceasefire while simultaneously asserting control over the Strait of Hormuz. China and Russia launched a joint naval exercise in the Pacific on the same morning NATO leaders prepare to gather in Ankara. And on the Eastern Front, Russia is feeding battlefield disinformation to Washington while Ukraine conducts strikes into Crimea. None of these are coincidentally timed.

The common thread: adversaries are reading U.S. attention as finite. When Washington is managing one crisis, others test the perimeter. That dynamic is no longer theoretical — it is the operating condition of July 2026.

## What We Know

**Iran War and Strait of Hormuz.** The United States and Israel initiated airstrikes against Iran on February 28, 2026. U.S. Central Command has struck over 11,000 targets inside Iran since the campaign began, at a reported cost to U.S. taxpayers of $113.3 billion as of mid-June. A two-week ceasefire took effect April 8 but came under strain when Israel struck targets in Lebanon and Iran refused to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. As of July 5, the Strait is operationally open — roughly 35 transits per day against a pre-war average near 110. Iran's Khatam ol Anbia Central Headquarters warned on July 2 that Iran will deliver a "decisive and swift" response to any U.S. interference with its management of the strait, and Tehran has announced it will charge transit fees on vessels using the Iranian-designated route. Qatar is serving as mediator; both sides agreed on July 1 to continue discussions, according to AP News.

**China-Russia Joint Naval Exercise.** China and Russia launched a joint naval exercise today, July 6, in waters near the Pacific, timed conspicuously as the NATO Ankara Summit opens tomorrow. The exercise involves combined fleet operations described by CGTN as a "maritime patrol." Mark Davidson, assistant professor of Asian studies and political science at Temple University Japan, told Stars and Stripes the exercise represents an "implicit challenge to a free and open Indo-Pacific," signaling combined strength at a moment when Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan "have reason to question America's capabilities and resolve."

**Ukraine-Russia Front.** The Institute for the Study of War's July 5 assessment notes that Putin likely used a recent call with Trump to present exaggerated claims of Russian battlefield advance, in an effort to shift U.S. perception of the war's trajectory. The ISW assesses that "Putin and other Kremlin officials are likely purposefully injecting" inflated success narratives as Ukrainian forces have demonstrated sustained strike capability against Russian military assets and energy infrastructure. Ukraine has been attacking Crimea with enough frequency that Russian authorities declared a state of emergency on the peninsula. Separately, Russian Ministry of Defense claims to have seized Lisne and Kopani (northwest of Hulyaipole) were refuted by Ukrainian units on July 2-3, with geolocated footage confirming Ukrainian forces remained in both locations.

**NATO Ankara Summit.** NATO's 32 member nations convene July 7-8 in Ankara. Key agenda items include defense spending commitments, the U.S. force posture in Europe (itself a point of friction this year), Ukraine's path, and the Iran war's impact on transatlantic cohesion. Former NATO Secretary General Anders Fogh Rasmussen has proposed a "coalition of the willing" — led by France and the United Kingdom as Europe's nuclear powers — to backstop continental defense, including Ukraine, independent of U.S. commitments. European leaders from five major defense powers met in Berlin last month to draft a shared defense strategy ahead of Ankara.

**U.S.-China Strategic Relationship.** The 14th World Peace Forum convened in Beijing today, July 6. Sun Yun, speaking at Tsinghua University's forum, noted that while the two governments have reached consensus on establishing a "constructive relationship of strategic stability," neither side has a shared definition of what that means in practice. A U.S. Supreme Court ruling in February 2026 struck down Trump's tariffs; the current tariff rate on Chinese goods stands at 30% with China's reciprocal rate at 10%, following a mid-2025 truce.

## What's Driving It

The Hormuz situation is primarily a leverage play. Iran cannot win a sustained air war against the United States and Israel. What it can do is hold global energy markets hostage. At 35 daily transits versus a pre-war baseline of 110, Iran has reduced global oil flow through the strait by roughly 68% — enough to sustain elevated energy prices without provoking a full naval confrontation. Iran's insistence on charging transit fees and designating approved shipping routes is an attempt to convert military deterrence into permanent institutional control of the world's most critical oil chokepoint.

The China-Russia naval exercise timing is deliberate. Beijing watches U.S. attention and resource deployment carefully. With CENTCOM absorbed in the Iran theater, European allies preoccupied with Ankara, and U.S. credibility under stress, both Beijing and Moscow see an opening to reinforce their partnership's military signaling without triggering a direct U.S. response. The ISW's assessment that PLA ADIZ sorties around Taiwan have declined from 300 to 200 monthly since early 2026 is not reassuring — it more likely reflects fleet maintenance and strategic recalibration than restraint.

Russia's information operation targeting Trump's assessment of the Ukraine war serves a specific goal: convince Washington that Russia is winning, that continued U.S. support is futile, and that a negotiated settlement should happen on Russian terms. The CSIS study released July 1 puts Russian casualties at 1.4 million killed or wounded since February 2022 — a number Russia will not acknowledge and that directly contradicts Kremlin narratives of strategic momentum.

At the NATO summit, the central fault line is not whether members support Ukraine but how much and in what form, given U.S. ambivalence about European security commitments, ongoing disputes over Greenland, and the Iran war drawing U.S. military resources and political attention away from Europe.

## Implications

**For U.S. National Security.** The three-front convergence is not coincidental. U.S. strategic bandwidth is being stress-tested simultaneously. CENTCOM is committed in the Iran theater; Pacific Command is watching China-Russia naval activity in its backyard; European Command faces a NATO ally restive about U.S. reliability. Each adversary is calibrating its moves against U.S. capacity to respond. The practical constraint is resourcing: U.S. defense industrial capacity remains a binding variable, and simultaneous demands in three theaters strain munitions stockpiles, logistics chains, and forward basing.

**For Businesses.** The Strait of Hormuz at 32% of pre-war throughput has restructured global energy logistics. Energy companies have rerouted through the Cape of Good Hope, adding 7-10 days to average transit times and significant fuel costs. The fee regime Iran is attempting to impose would create a permanent toll structure that no major energy importer wants to legitimize. Shipping insurance rates for Gulf transit remain elevated. Companies with supply chains dependent on Gulf petrochemicals and feedstocks face continued cost pressure.

**For Allies.** Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan are watching the China-Russia exercise and drawing conclusions about the reliability of U.S. extended deterrence — conclusions that will shape their own defense spending and bilateral security arrangements. For European allies, the Ankara summit is a stress test for alliance cohesion. France and the UK's emerging "coalition of the willing" framework is a hedge, not a replacement, for NATO — but it signals that European capitals are no longer treating U.S. commitment as given.

## What to Watch

**Iran Ceasefire Negotiations.** The Qatar-mediated talks are the critical variable. Watch whether Iran formalizes its fee structure for Hormuz transit and whether the U.S. accepts or challenges it. Any U.S. naval confrontation with IRGC vessels enforcing the fee regime would escalate the conflict. The absence of confrontation would implicitly validate Iranian sovereignty claims over international waters.

**NATO Ankara Outcomes.** The summit closes July 8. Watch for concrete defense spending commitments above the 2% GDP threshold, any language on Ukraine membership pathways, and whether the communiqué addresses the Iran war's impact on transatlantic burden-sharing.

**PLA ADIZ Activity Around Taiwan.** ISW assessed a reduction in sorties; watch whether that pattern continues or reverses in the coming weeks, particularly as the China-Russia naval exercise concludes. A resumption of high-volume ADIZ operations would signal Beijing is recalibrating its Taiwan pressure campaign.

**Putin-Trump Communications.** The ISW's assessment that Putin is feeding inflated claims to the Trump administration to shape U.S. policy is a warning flag. Watch for any U.S. statements that echo Russian casualty or territorial claims that contradict ISW and Ukrainian military reporting.

**Hormuz Transit Volumes.** A sustained increase above 50 daily transits would indicate a stabilizing ceasefire. A drop back toward zero would signal escalation. Either way, the baseline is not returning to 110.
