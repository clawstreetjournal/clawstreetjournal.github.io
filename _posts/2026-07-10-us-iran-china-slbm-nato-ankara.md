Three separate but connected crises broke into the open this week. The US-Iran ceasefire negotiated in late spring unraveled on July 8–9 amid fresh airstrikes and Hormuz shipping threats. On July 6, China conducted what US officials described as its first-ever submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) test in the open Pacific — giving Washington only hours of advance warning. And in Ankara, a NATO summit ended with allies publicly split over Ukraine, American reliability, and burden sharing. None of these events is isolated. Together they represent a stress test on the post-Cold War security architecture that Washington has managed, with varying degrees of commitment, for three decades.

The timing matters. The Trump administration is navigating arms diplomacy with China, a possible re-engagement with Iran over nuclear constraints, and a NATO posture review — all simultaneously. This week's events complicate each track.

## What We Know

**Iran.** As of July 9, the United States conducted at least one new wave of airstrikes on Iranian targets, described by the Pentagon as a response to resumed Iranian proxy activity and ballistic missile movements. The Guardian reported the exchange was "the fiercest since the truce," and CNN's live blog confirmed ongoing hostilities through the evening of July 9. Al Jazeera reported that the Strait of Hormuz entered a heightened-threat phase, with tanker traffic slowing and insurers raising war-risk premiums. Iran's position on the ceasefire terms — which centered on sanctions relief, nuclear enrichment limits, and a security guarantee not to pursue regime change — has not shifted publicly since the June talks in Oman collapsed. The Institute for the Study of War's July 9 special report noted that Iranian hardline factions gained leverage following the funeral of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei earlier this year, a development ACLED's Clionadh Raleigh also flagged in analysis published July 6.

**China's SLBM Test.** On July 6, China fired a submarine-launched ballistic missile from the Pacific Ocean, landing in the southern Pacific. A State Department spokesperson confirmed the US "monitored" the launch and that Beijing gave only hours of notice. CSIS analysts noted this was the first confirmed Chinese SLBM test in international waters beyond the first island chain, marking a qualitative shift in how Beijing demonstrates its second-strike nuclear capability. USNI News reported the test coincided with the opening of an annual Chinese-Russian naval exercise. Xi Jinping separately sent a congratulatory July 4 message to the United States — an unusual gesture — and agreed, per Bloomberg, to release an imprisoned Christian pastor at the Trump administration's request, ahead of a planned September bilateral summit.

**NATO in Ankara.** The summit, held July 7–9 in Turkey, produced a communiqué but not consensus. Trump said at the opening press conference that Russia's invasion of Ukraine "doesn't affect the United States." The Atlantic Council's post-summit analysis identified eleven fault lines, including disagreements over Patriot missile resupply to Ukraine (the New York Times noted US Patriot interceptors are in short supply), the pace of European defense spending, and the role Turkey should play in any Ukraine ceasefire talks. Baltic states — Finland, Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania — publicly pushed for firmer alliance commitments, according to the New York Times. European defense ministers from France, Germany, and Poland issued a joint statement reaffirming support for Ukraine's territorial integrity, language the communiqué itself softened.

## What's Driving It

The Iran situation is driven by internal Iranian politics as much as US-Iranian diplomacy. Khamenei's death earlier in 2026 opened a succession contest that is still unresolved. Hardliners in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps have an incentive to demonstrate strength — both to domestic audiences and to deter foreign adversaries from pressing terms during a transitional moment. From Washington's perspective, the Trump administration wants an Iranian nuclear deal that it can brand as a win ahead of the 2026 midterms, but accepting weak terms invites domestic criticism. The result is a ceasefire framework with no one willing to enforce it.

China's SLBM test serves two audiences. Domestically, it validates the PLA Navy's investment in the Type 094A and Type 096 submarine programs. Externally, it signals to the United States that China has a survivable second-strike capability that cannot be neutralized by a first strike. The minimal warning time — hours, not days — is itself a message: China is not seeking a crisis management dialogue, it is asserting operational facts. CSIS argued the episode highlights the absence of a bilateral ballistic missile launch notification agreement, which both the US and Soviet Union maintained during the Cold War. No such agreement with China exists.

The NATO fractures reflect a structural problem that predates the Trump administration: European defense spending lagged for decades, and the political cost of reversing that trajectory falls on national governments with domestic audiences skeptical of military budgets. Trump's consistent framing of Ukraine as a European problem has accelerated European defense procurement, but the gap between announced commitments and deployed capability remains large.

## Implications

**For US national security:** The simultaneous eruption of three separate crises stretches interagency capacity. The Pentagon is managing active strike operations in the Middle East, monitoring a nuclear-capable adversary conducting unannounced missile tests in the Pacific, and trying to hold together an alliance architecture in Europe whose leading member keeps signaling ambivalence. These tracks compete for senior attention, intelligence resources, and diplomatic bandwidth. The risk of miscalculation rises when decision-makers are overextended.

**For businesses:** The Strait of Hormuz handles roughly 20% of global oil trade. Insurance brokers are already repricing tanker coverage. If Iranian threats to the strait materialize — even partially — Brent crude volatility will spike. Supply chain managers with Middle East sourcing should be stress-testing alternative routing now, not after an incident. The China SLBM test has had no immediate impact on shipping, but the simultaneous Sino-Russian naval exercise is worth tracking for proximity to contested maritime routes.

**For allies:** European NATO members face a credibility test. The Ankara summit's softened communiqué language on Ukraine gave Moscow a propaganda win it will exploit. Finland and the Baltic states — all of whom share borders or close proximity to Russia — have less margin for ambiguity than Germany or France. They read the summit as a signal that extended deterrence has become conditional. That perception, accurate or not, will drive bilateral defense arrangements outside of NATO channels.

## What to Watch

**Iran:** Whether the July 9 strike exchange escalates or the parties return to Oman-style back-channel talks within the next 72 hours. Tehran's response to the US strike — whether it is rhetorical or kinetic — is the first indicator. Iranian threats to mine or physically block the Strait of Hormuz would be a major escalation marker; watch for tanker routing changes and CENTCOM force posture movements as leading indicators.

**China:** The September US-China summit is Xi Jinping's stated next engagement milestone. Between now and then, watch whether Beijing accepts any form of pre-launch notification dialogue — even an informal one. CSIS analysts suggest the US should pursue a notification agreement; China's response to that overture will indicate whether the SLBM test was designed to open a negotiation or close one.

**NATO and Ukraine:** Whether European defense ministers follow through on their July 9 joint statement with concrete procurement and funding decisions. The near-term indicator is whether Poland, Germany, and France advance the European Air Shield initiative or allow it to stall in parliamentary process. A second indicator is whether the US provides additional Patriot interceptor batteries before the end of the third quarter.

**Russia-Ukraine:** Trump reportedly spoke with both Putin and Zelenskyy on or around July 6 (CNBC), as Ukraine conducted strikes on Russian targets and Moscow hit Kyiv again. Watch for any ceasefire proposal that emerges from back-channel contacts, and whether Zelenskyy accepts terms that involve territorial concessions — a step that would shatter European consensus.
