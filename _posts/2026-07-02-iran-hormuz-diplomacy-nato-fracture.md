On the morning of July 2, the global security picture is defined by three simultaneous stress tests: indirect U.S.-Iran talks in Doha that are progressing without breakthrough, a massive Russian missile-and-drone barrage that killed at least 11 people in Kyiv overnight, and a NATO alliance arriving at its Ankara summit with a structural question that neither communiqué language nor defense spending pledges has fully resolved — whether European members can credibly fill the roles Washington is quietly vacating.

None of these crises is new. All three are entering a phase where the next several days will determine whether they stabilize, expand, or collide with each other in ways planners have not fully modeled.

## What We Know

**Iran and the Strait of Hormuz.** U.S. and Iranian negotiators concluded a second round of indirect talks in Doha on July 2 through Qatari mediators. Qatar's Foreign Ministry characterized the outcome as "positive progress" and confirmed that a third round is scheduled after the funeral of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who is to be buried July 9. Vice President Vance told CNN on July 1 that talks are "going well" and that the nuclear issue is now formally on the table. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi simultaneously warned of an "immediate powerful response" to any Israeli strike and reiterated demands that the United States freeze Iranian financial assets and acknowledge Iranian sovereignty over the strait. According to ISW's July 1 special report, Tehran is pushing for both conditions as preconditions to a lasting arrangement, not as post-deal incentives. MarineTraffic data recorded at least 35 commercial vessels transiting the Strait in the 24 hours ending July 1 — traffic is moving, but the underlying legal dispute is not resolved.

**Russia-Ukraine.** Russian forces launched 74 missiles and 496 drones at Ukraine overnight, with Kyiv bearing the heaviest impact. Ukrainian officials confirmed at least 11 dead in Kyiv and 13 killed in the broader overnight campaign across Ukraine. Poland scrambled fighter jets and Finland restricted airspace in response to spillover concerns. The Kyiv Independent reported six dead and 111 injured across Ukraine in the prior 24 hours from separate drone attacks. The Institute for the Study of War's June 25 assessment confirmed that U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio acknowledged no agreement had been reached to end the war. Ukrainian drone campaigns have, separately, disrupted roughly 40 percent of Russia's oil export capacity by targeting Novorossiysk, Primorsk, and Ust-Luga, according to Reuters calculations from March 2026.

**China-Japan.** Beijing accused Japan of "reckless new militarism" on July 2, while Tokyo protested what it described as Chinese airspace incursions. AP News reported that China placed 40 Japanese entities on an export control watchlist as relations deteriorated. The Guardian noted that a dispute over Japan's aircraft carrier conversion program has become a recurring flashpoint. Taiwan's President Lai Ching-te warned graduating military cadets on July 1 to resist Chinese infiltration and espionage operations targeting the armed forces. ISW's June 26 China-Taiwan update noted that the PRC released footage of hypersonic missile tests, which ISW analysts assessed as a deliberate signal that PLA capabilities are qualitatively different from Iran's — and therefore that U.S. missile defense performance in the Middle East should not be extrapolated to a Taiwan scenario.

**European Security.** A NATO source told Reuters on July 1 that European members have filled "almost all" the gaps left by the United States in alliance defense plans, ahead of the July 7–8 Ankara summit. Foreign Policy's June 29 analysis, citing EU officials, found that European governments have stopped expecting coordinated handoff procedures from the Trump administration and are war-gaming how Article 42.7 of the EU Treaty — the bloc's collective defense clause — might be invoked independently of NATO if the alliance proves paralyzed. The Guardian reported that NATO leaders attending Ankara are concerned that U.S. troop commitments may be contingent on the personal relationships individual European leaders maintain with the White House.

**U.S.-China Bilateral.** Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi stated on July 2 that China and the United States should "remove disruptions, overcome obstacles, and stay firmly on the right course toward building a constructive bilateral relationship of strategic stability." China Briefing noted a significant data point: China's imports of U.S. sorghum surged over 1,000 percent in the first two months of 2026, reaching $260 million in value, suggesting selective agricultural concessions are functioning as a stabilizer in the trade relationship. South China Morning Post analysts assessed that the personal dynamic between the two heads of state may be the relationship's weakest structural element going into the second half of 2026.

## What's Driving It

The Hormuz situation has a specific internal deadline: Khamenei's July 9 funeral will reshape Iranian domestic politics in ways that are not yet clear. The clerical establishment and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps will be navigating succession, and neither party in Doha knows how that will affect Iranian negotiating authority afterward. Iran's demand for formal U.S. acknowledgment of control over the strait is not simply a bargaining chip — it is a domestic-political requirement that any successor government will need to validate its legitimacy. That makes it structurally difficult to concede even in a deal that gives Tehran everything else it wants.

Russia's escalation posture reflects two simultaneous pressures: Ukrainian drone success against oil infrastructure has damaged the Russian state budget at a time when the central bank governor's recent absence from public events — noted by the New York Times — has raised speculation about elite tensions at the top of Russia's political economy. Hitting Kyiv hard during ceasefire-adjacent diplomacy has, historically, served as a signal to European capitals that any peace deal must be negotiated on Moscow's terms, not Kyiv's.

China's moves against Japan are partly driven by the Japan-U.S. security treaty's evolution. Japan's conversion of two Izumo-class ships into carriers capable of operating F-35Bs represents a qualitative step that Beijing has chosen to treat as a threshold event, similar to how it treated South Korea's THAAD deployment in 2016. Export controls on 40 Japanese entities are a graduated economic tool that doesn't cross into direct military confrontation but signals Beijing's willingness to weaponize supply chains selectively.

NATO's drift is structural and has been accelerating since early 2025. European members are not "filling gaps" out of strength — they are improvising under time pressure because the alternative is a collective defense posture with acknowledged holes. The decision by EU officials to game out Article 42.7 activation is significant precisely because invoking it would mean conceding that NATO's Article 5 guarantee is no longer reliable.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security:** The Doha talks represent a real near-term opportunity to reduce risk in the Persian Gulf, but the post-Khamenei political transition creates a narrow window. If a deal isn't locked in before Iranian domestic politics resets, the next negotiating partner in Tehran may have less authority and more incentive to reopen settled points. Meanwhile, Russia's continued large-scale strikes against Ukrainian cities while U.S.-Russia diplomacy stalls will increase pressure on the administration from European allies who see the two tracks as contradictory.

**For businesses:** The Strait of Hormuz carried roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG trade before the 2026 crisis. Shipping traffic is currently flowing, but insurers are pricing political risk accordingly. The China-Japan export control expansion directly affects semiconductor and advanced materials supply chains — companies sourcing components from Japanese entities now on Beijing's watchlist face potential disruption. U.S. sorghum's 1,000 percent volume surge into China is a genuine positive signal for agricultural exporters, but it reflects selective Chinese policy, not a structural normalization.

**For allies:** European NATO members are making real defense investments, but the Foreign Policy assessment suggests those investments are being made in a political environment where the U.S. commitment is treated as genuinely contingent. The Ankara summit will produce language emphasizing solidarity; it will not resolve the question of whether Washington is a reliable guarantor of European security. Taiwan's government is operating with an explicit awareness that it is being watched by Beijing as a different class of target than what Iran represented.

## What to Watch

**July 7–8:** NATO Ankara summit. Watch whether a specific burden-sharing framework with timelines is announced, or whether the communiqué remains aspirational. The gap between those two outcomes matters.

**July 9:** Khamenei's funeral and immediate aftermath. The Iranian negotiating team's behavior in the days following will be the first real signal of how succession dynamics affect the Hormuz track. Any resumption of Iranian activity in the strait in that window — even incidents framed as accidental — should be read as a domestic political signal, not a negotiating move.

**Russia oil revenue:** The cumulative effect of Ukrainian strikes on Novorossiysk, Primorsk, and Ust-Luga has removed approximately 40 percent of Russia's western oil export capacity. If that figure holds or increases into Q3, watch for Russian budget pressure to surface in procurement and manpower indicators — a potential constraint on the pace of large-scale attacks.

**China-Japan:** Whether Tokyo files a formal diplomatic protest at the UN over Chinese airspace violations and whether Beijing escalates export controls beyond 40 entities will determine whether this is a managed signaling episode or the beginning of a sustained economic coercion campaign.

**U.S.-China bilateral:** The July communications coming from Chinese Foreign Ministry channels are notably conciliatory in tone while operational military pressure on Japan and Taiwan continues. That gap between diplomatic language and operational behavior is the tension to track through the back half of 2026.
