Two more American service members are dead. One is missing. A base in Jordan came under Iranian fire on Friday, July 18 — the latest in a series of IRGC-directed strikes that have now killed 16 U.S. military personnel since the United States and Israel launched operations against Iran in February. By Saturday morning, U.S. Central Command had already responded with its eighth consecutive night of airstrikes inside Iran.

That this is now treated as routine is the real story. The Iran conflict has settled into a rhythmic exchange of fire that neither side appears ready to stop. Meanwhile, China quietly confirmed a submarine-launched ballistic missile test in the Pacific — the first public acknowledgment of such a capability in years. And in Ankara, NATO allies just concluded a summit that amounted to a structural renegotiation of the transatlantic security bargain.

Any one of these developments would dominate a normal week. All three are happening at once.

## What We Know

**Iran theater:** CENTCOM confirmed on July 18 that two U.S. service members were killed and one remains missing following an Iranian attack on a base in Jordan. The attack brings total American military fatalities in the conflict to 16, with more than 400 service members injured since February, according to CENTCOM. The IRGC announced on July 11 that it had struck a Cyprus-flagged vessel and briefly closed the Strait of Hormuz. The Institute for the Study of War assessed in its July 13 special report that the U.S.-Iran ceasefire has "effectively ended with relatively more intense and consistent exchanges of fire in the Persian Gulf." Oil markets responded: Brent crude climbed sharply after the Trump administration announced Iranian ships would no longer be permitted transit through the strait. WTI approaching $90 is now being tracked as a live outcome on prediction markets.

**China SLBM test:** On July 6, PRC state media confirmed that an unspecified ballistic missile nuclear submarine (SSBN) had conducted a submarine-launched ballistic missile (SLBM) test. ISW's July 10 China-Taiwan update assessed the launch was likely intended to demonstrate a credible nuclear second-strike capability. Maritime Safety Administration notices appeared in both the Bohai Sea and South China Sea, leaving the precise launch corridor ambiguous. The test drew concern from the United States, Australia, Japan, and New Zealand. CSIS analysts argued this week that the test underscores the need for a bilateral missile launch notification agreement.

**South China Sea coalition:** On July 12, a 14-nation coalition including the United States, United Kingdom, and Romania issued a joint statement reaffirming the 2016 Permanent Court of Arbitration ruling that rejected China's expansive maritime claims. The statement was notable both for its breadth and for the inclusion of Romania, a sign that European nations are treating Indo-Pacific stability as directly linked to their own security calculations.

**NATO Ankara Summit:** The alliance concluded its summit in Ankara, Turkey, in early July. European allies departed with commitments rather than assurances. Lockheed Martin and Rheinmetall announced plans to manufacture ATACMS missiles in Germany — the first time the system will be produced outside the United States. NATO committed $40 billion to a new Drone Edge Initiative, and Germany separately funded 50,000 AI-guided first-person-view drones for Ukraine, according to reporting in Defense News and Tech Times.

**Ukraine-Russia:** ISW's July 13 assessment noted that Ukraine has intensified and expanded its strike campaign targeting Russian logistics and fuel transport to Crimea. Separately, Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski confirmed recent U.S. warnings that Russia may be planning provocations against unspecified NATO member states. Ukraine shot down cruise missiles and drones in the July 6 attack but lacked sufficient interceptors to stop a single ballistic missile, according to President Zelensky.

**US-China trade and technology:** Mondaq's July 2026 trade law roundup identified a growing legal conflict between China's new counter-extraterritoriality regulations and the Trump administration's enhanced customs enforcement strategy. Multinational firms now face directly competing legal obligations from two governments.

## What's Driving It

The Iran conflict originated in the collapse of nuclear negotiations and a prior air engagement in 2025. The current phase is sustained by mutual deterrence failure: neither side has found a cost-benefit threshold that compels withdrawal. Iran cannot cede the Strait of Hormuz without surrendering its most potent economic coercion tool. The United States has invested too much military credibility to accept a draw that leaves Iranian nuclear infrastructure intact.

China's SLBM test is better understood as a capability advertisement than a provocation. Beijing is watching U.S. military attention fragment across Iran, Ukraine support, and the broader Indo-Pacific. A demonstrated second-strike capability raises the cost of any American escalation calculus against China — including over Taiwan. The May summit between Trump and Xi produced what Foreign Policy described as a "sincere desire to resume robust trade relations," but that diplomatic tone coexists with China's strategic preparations for a scenario in which diplomacy breaks down.

NATO's post-Ankara trajectory reflects a structural adjustment rather than a crisis. For years, European defense spending was a political argument. Now it is a procurement reality. The Lockheed-Rheinmetall ATACMS joint venture represents exactly the kind of defense industrial integration that U.S. officials have demanded for two decades. The Ankara summit codified the end of unconditional American security guarantees, replacing them with a conditional partnership framework in which European contributions are expected, tracked, and priced into the alliance's operational planning.

Russia's willingness to threaten NATO member provocations while absorbing continued Ukrainian strikes on Crimea supply lines suggests Moscow is attempting to fracture Western unity at the margins rather than escalate directly. The threat of provocations is itself a coercion tool — designed to force European capitals to hedge rather than deepen Ukraine support.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security:** The American military is simultaneously sustaining an active air campaign against Iran, deterring Chinese aggression in the Pacific, supporting Ukrainian air defense, and absorbing pressure on Jordanian basing. Each commitment is individually manageable; the combination strains munitions stockpiles, airlift capacity, and institutional attention. The Hormuz closure — even partial — complicates Pacific Fleet logistics at the worst possible moment given Chinese nuclear signaling.

**For businesses:** Companies operating in the Gulf face disrupted shipping lanes and sharply higher insurance premiums. The Hormuz constraint is pushing crude prices toward ranges that pressure refining margins globally. Separately, firms with supply chains touching both the United States and China now face a dual compliance trap: U.S. export controls expanding on one side, Chinese counter-extraterritoriality enforcement tightening on the other. Companies that have not mapped their exposure to both legal regimes are now past the point where deferral is viable.

**For allies:** The Ankara commitments signal that European defense investment is real and accelerating. The France Diplomatie Bastille Day framing — "Europe's strategic awakening" — is not rhetoric; it tracks procurement data. Japan and Australia are recalibrating their own deterrence math in response to China's SLBM demonstration. India is expanding missile sales across the Indo-Pacific, signaling that New Delhi sees Chinese assertiveness as a sustained commercial and strategic opportunity.

## What to Watch

**Iran escalation floor:** The next 72 hours will test whether the Jordan base attack triggers a meaningful U.S. escalatory response beyond the current airstrike pattern, or whether the exchange settles into another tactical cycle. A third American death or a strike on a higher-profile asset would force a decision on whether to pursue Iranian leadership targets — a threshold the administration has not crossed.

**Strait of Hormuz transit status:** Watch whether commercial shipping resumes through the strait under the current exchange conditions. Sustained Hormuz restriction above 10 days begins to produce supply disruptions in Asia that become politically unmanageable for Japan, South Korea, and India.

**China SLBM follow-on:** CSIS called for a bilateral missile launch notification agreement. Whether Beijing responds to that proposal or ignores it will indicate whether the SLBM test was a deterrent signal (amenable to dialogue) or the beginning of normalized operational testing (not amenable).

**Russia-NATO border incidents:** Poland's confirmation of U.S. warnings about potential Russian provocations against NATO states creates a live tripwire. Any confirmed Russian action against Baltic or Polish infrastructure — cyber or physical — would force a formal Article 4 consultation and potentially Article 5 deliberations.

**US-China regulatory collision:** Watch for the first enforcement action under China's counter-extraterritoriality rules against a U.S.-affiliated company. That will be the test case for whether the competing regulatory frameworks produce a crisis or a negotiated carve-out.
