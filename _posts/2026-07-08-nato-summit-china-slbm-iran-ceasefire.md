Three things happened in the 72 hours before Wednesday morning that, taken separately, might rate a single news cycle each. Taken together, they define the security environment heading into the second half of 2026.

Russia struck Kyiv for the second time in under a week, timed to the opening of the NATO summit in Ankara. China fired a submarine-launched ballistic missile—a JL-2—from a nuclear-powered submarine into the South Pacific, flying the trajectory over the Philippines. And the United States and Iran agreed, through Qatar as mediator, to continue negotiations over Iran's nuclear program and the contested terms of a Strait of Hormuz ceasefire. None of these events is isolated. Each reflects the same structural condition: multiple powers are testing resolve simultaneously, and the United States is trying to manage all of them at once.

## What We Know

**Russia–Ukraine / NATO Ankara**

Russia conducted a major strike on Kyiv on July 5, two days after U.S. intelligence warnings reported by *The Daily Telegraph* and *The Independent* that Moscow may be planning a provocation or limited ground incursion into Poland, potentially using forces from Kaliningrad or Belarus. The Russian Ministry of Defense stated explicitly that it would increase the number and intensity of strikes against Ukraine in response to continued Western drone, missile, and ammunition deliveries—a statement assessed by the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) as timed to deter NATO allies meeting in Ankara from expanding support. A separate IISS report published this week documented a 19-month Russian drone campaign, running August 2024 through February 2026, that sent 144 low-cost UAVs over sensitive sites in a dozen NATO member states plus Ireland, including bases hosting U.S. nuclear weapons.

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky attended the Ankara summit on July 7, with stated objectives of securing additional air defense systems and stronger diplomatic commitments. Ukraine simultaneously struck dozens of Russia's "shadow fleet" vessels with drone attacks.

**China's SLBM Test and Sino-American Diplomatic Track**

On July 6, the People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) test-launched a JL-2 submarine-launched ballistic missile from a nuclear-powered submarine in the South China Sea, with the missile flying over the Philippines before landing in the South Pacific. Simultaneously, China and Russia kicked off their annual joint naval exercise. Taiwan's National Security Council Secretary-General Joseph Wu described the launch on X as "a provocation that destabilizes the #IndoPacific." Senator Tammy Duckworth traveled to Taiwan on July 7 to reaffirm U.S. security commitments, warning against any concessions to Beijing and highlighting Taiwan's role in semiconductor supply chains and regional stability—a visit that comes amid Congressional concern over the Trump administration's posture following the May U.S.-China summit.

That summit yielded a trade framework under which China committed to purchasing at least $17 billion annually in U.S. agricultural products through 2028. Bloomberg reported July 2 that both sides are targeting further tariff reductions on agricultural goods. Both governments have since described the relationship as one of "constructive strategic stability"—language the Princeton School of Public and International Affairs characterized on July 7 as a meaningful rhetorical shift from the previous adversarial framing.

**Iran and the Strait of Hormuz**

AP News reported July 1 that the United States and Iran, through Qatar as intermediary, agreed to continue discussions following talks on the Strait of Hormuz and Iran's nuclear program. ISW's Iran Update for July 4 documented Iran continuing to use threats to compel commercial vessels to transit through its asserted "illegal traffic separation scheme" in the strait. Iran is separately pushing Washington to unfreeze a significant amount of Iranian financial assets and to acknowledge Iranian control of the strait—the latter a demand the United States has not publicly accepted. An ongoing ceasefire in Gaza, established under UN Security Council Resolution 2803 of November 2025, is holding, with a Board of Peace headed by the United States governing Gaza as a transitional administration. However, ACLED's July 2026 Middle East overview assessed that fundamental incompatibilities over Hamas disarmament, Israeli troop withdrawal, and post-war governance remain unresolved.

## What's Driving It

**Russia's pre-summit strikes are not battlefield necessity.** They are coercive signaling—an attempt to raise the cost of NATO solidarity before leaders sign communiqués. The Kremlin's calculation is consistent: large strikes timed to major Western diplomatic events have recurred through both the August 2025 Alaska summit and multiple 2025–2026 bilateral talks. The Poland threat intelligence, if accurate, fits the same pattern: pressure the alliance's eastern flank to keep it reactive rather than forward-postured.

**China's SLBM test is a capability demonstration with three audiences.** The first is the United States, which needs to assess whether China's submarine-based nuclear second-strike capability has matured. The second is Taiwan and other regional partners—conducting the test over Philippine airspace without advance notice was a deliberate signal. The third audience is domestic: China is running its annual joint exercise with Russia simultaneously, reinforcing the alignment narrative at a moment when U.S.-China trade diplomacy is producing modest commercial wins. Beijing appears to be compartmentalizing—pursuing economic accommodation on soybeans while signaling military independence on nuclear forces.

**Iran's position reflects a post-ceasefire bargaining window.** With the Gaza conflict temporarily contained and Hezbollah forces in southern Lebanon attempting reconstitution (per Israeli security officials cited by ISW on July 6), Tehran is navigating a constrained environment. The nuclear talks are real but slow. Iran's demands—frozen asset release plus Strait of Hormuz recognition—are maximalist negotiating positions, not final offers, but they signal that Tehran has not abandoned leverage.

## Implications

For **U.S. national security**, the convergence of three simultaneous pressure campaigns is the core problem. Managing Russia's escalation threats, China's nuclear signaling, and Iran's negotiations concurrently strains diplomatic bandwidth. The Trump administration's trade-first posture toward Beijing has not arrested Chinese military assertiveness. The IISS finding that Russian drones overflew NATO nuclear weapon sites for 19 months without triggering a formal allied response is a deterrence credibility gap that the Ankara summit will need to address directly.

For **businesses and supply chains**, the JL-2 test over Philippine airspace raises the risk profile for shipping through the South China Sea and the Pacific corridors beyond it. Iran's continued coercion in the Strait of Hormuz—through which roughly 20% of global oil trade passes—remains a material exposure for energy markets. The China-U.S. agricultural framework offers a narrow cushion for American farm exporters, but it remains contingent on political relationships that the SLBM test has strained.

For **U.S. allies**, Ankara's communiqué language on Russia and Poland will be read as a test of the alliance's Article 5 credibility. South Korea, Japan, and Australia are watching the U.S. congressional response to the SLBM test closely. Taiwan's government publicly described the launch as destabilizing; Washington's public response, beyond Duckworth's visit, remains muted as of this writing.

## What to Watch

**NATO Ankara outcomes (July 8–9):** Watch whether the summit produces binding defense spending commitments above 2% of GDP and whether Poland formally requests additional force posture adjustments. A weak communiqué on Article 5 language would be read in Moscow as a green light for further escalation.

**U.S. response to the SLBM test:** A formal State Department or Pentagon statement characterizing the test as destabilizing—or the absence of one—will signal how much diplomatic space the administration is willing to preserve with Beijing. Silence carries its own message.

**Iran talks in Qatar (ongoing):** Watch for movement on the frozen-assets question. If the United States agrees to partial unfreezing, it signals a deal is closer. If talks break down over the Strait of Hormuz sovereignty demand, expect Iran to resume assertive vessel interdictions.

**Hezbollah reconstitution in southern Lebanon:** ISW's July 6 report noted IDF encirclement of Hezbollah positions in Bint Jbeil District and Nabatieh Governorate. Whether that encirclement holds or triggers a resumption of combat is the near-term tripwire for regional re-escalation.

**China-Russia joint naval exercise:** The exercise, running concurrently with the SLBM test, is worth tracking for scope and declared objectives. Exercises that practice power-projection toward Japan or the Ryukyu chain would indicate a qualitative step up from previous iterations.
