Three active military confrontations are running simultaneously this week, and the connective tissue between them is fraying. The U.S.-Iran ceasefire—extended indefinitely by the White House in April—died quietly on July 8 when the Trump administration declared it over. Within five days, U.S. Central Command reinstated a naval blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, resumed airstrike campaigns targeting Iranian command infrastructure, and watched as Iran struck back against commercial vessels. Fifty-six confirmed maritime attacks have been logged as of July 15, with 17 seafarer fatalities reported by shipping intelligence firm Kpler. On July 16, only 13 vessels transited the Strait in a single day—a waterway that normally handles roughly 20 percent of global oil trade.

Meanwhile, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky fired his 35-year-old defense minister, Mykhailo Fedorov, triggering mass street protests in Kyiv. China's People's Liberation Army Navy tested a submarine-launched ballistic missile in the South Pacific on July 6 and opened a joint naval exercise with Russia near Qingdao that same week. None of these events are coincidental in timing, even if they aren't directly coordinated. Adversaries watch each other's distractions, and there are three to choose from right now.

## What We Know

**Iran Theater.** The Trump administration declared the ceasefire terminated on July 8. On July 12, the White House announced a naval blockade of Iranian ports within the Strait of Hormuz and floated—then within 48 hours retracted—a proposal to impose a 20 percent toll on commercial shipping transiting the waterway. That toll was dropped after Gulf state partners objected, and the administration said it would instead be replaced by increased Gulf state investment commitments to the United States. By July 14, the blockade was in effect. U.S. CENTCOM confirmed new strike waves on July 15 and 16, targeting Iranian air defense sites, missile storage, and command centers. At least one tanker attempting to bypass the blockade was hit.

The backstory matters: the ceasefire was first declared after the initial U.S.-Israel strike campaign against Iran that began February 28. The Islamabad talks between Washington and Tehran collapsed on April 12, after which JD Vance announced the failure publicly. The extended ceasefire held tenuously until early July before sequential violations by both sides ended it. Iran's Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei died during the conflict; his funeral reportedly featured explicit calls for retaliation.

**Ukraine.** Zelensky dismissed Fedorov, who had become publicly synonymous with Ukraine's drone warfare program, as part of a broader government reshuffle on July 16. The firing sparked street protests across Ukraine. Fedorov publicly attacked what he called the military's "old guard" after his dismissal. Ukraine's Armed Forces denied a Russian claim of capturing the eastern city of Kostiantynivka on July 4. An anonymous Russian general speaking to journalist Dmitry Kolezev on July 6 alleged that Russian military command is systematically inflating battlefield reports to Moscow—an assessment corroborated by the Institute for the Study of War's July 9 offensive campaign assessment, which judged Russia unable to win a war of attrition.

**Indo-Pacific.** The PLA Navy conducted a submarine-launched ballistic missile test, with PRC Maritime Safety Administration notices placed in both the Bohai Sea and the South China Sea on July 6—suggesting either operational ambiguity or deliberate signaling. ISW's July 10 China-Taiwan update assessed the test as designed to demonstrate an enhanced nuclear second-strike capability. China and Russia opened their Joint Sea-2026 naval exercise on July 6, deploying ten vessels including surface, underwater, aerial, and support units near Qingdao. The exercise concluded with what Chinese state media described as a "powerful naval show of force." Separately, the U.S. deployed six 154-foot Coast Guard fast-response cutters previously stationed in the Middle East to Singapore and the Philippines, responding to what officials called China's "gray-zone" campaign in the South China Sea.

**European Security.** The NATO Ankara Summit, held earlier this month, produced a declaration noting that European members and Canada had increased defense investments by more than $139 billion. EU total military expenditure reached €418 billion in 2025 and is projected to hit €454 billion in 2026, per the European Defence Agency. But an EDA report released July 16 found that higher spending has not improved the efficiency of the EU defense industrial base—a structural problem that spending alone cannot solve. Finland and the Baltic states remain particularly concerned about the reliability of Article 5 commitments under the current U.S. administration.

## What's Driving It

The Iran escalation has a visible economic logic. Iran's continued support for Houthi and proxy attacks on Gulf shipping was eroding what little diplomatic credibility the ceasefire had. The Trump administration calculated that a resumed blockade, combined with Gulf state investment pledges as a substitute for the Hormuz toll, could extract financial concessions while maintaining maximum economic pressure on Tehran. That calculation may prove correct, but it treats the Strait of Hormuz as a lever with clean, predictable effects—which it is not.

China's SLBM test is best understood as communication, not operational necessity. Beijing is watching the U.S. military consumed on two fronts—Ukraine support and active Iran operations—and signaling to Washington that any Taiwan contingency planning must account for a credible Chinese nuclear second-strike. The Joint Sea-2026 exercise with Russia reinforces the message. Foreign Policy's July 14 analysis of Chinese diplomatic strategy notes that Beijing has developed a layered toolkit for retaliation: export controls on critical minerals, the Unreliable Entity List, diplomatic sanctions against U.S. officials, and targeted pressure on defense contractors. Beijing is deploying these tools calibrated to stop short of triggering a formal response.

Russia's motivation in Ukraine is straightforwardly attrition—absorb losses, hold territory, wait for Western political will to erode. The anonymous general's claim of inflated battlefield reporting, if credible, suggests the Kremlin is managing domestic perception rather than winning territory. Fedorov's dismissal in Ukraine may reflect internal political pressure on Zelensky rather than a change in military doctrine, but the street protests signal that the drone program had genuine popular support—possibly more than the government that built it.

## Implications

**For U.S. national security,** the simultaneous Iran and Ukraine commitments are straining force posture. Moving Coast Guard cutters from the Middle East to the Pacific is a visible sign of constrained assets. CENTCOM is conducting sustained strike operations against Iran while the Navy enforces a blockade in one of the world's most contested waterways. Any escalation in a third theater—Taiwan, Korea, the South China Sea—would arrive during an extended operational stretch.

**For businesses,** the Hormuz blockade is the most immediate material risk. With only 13 ships transiting on July 16, shipping costs for Gulf oil, LNG, and cargo are rising sharply. Insurance premiums for vessels in the region have been climbing since June. Companies with supply chains dependent on Gulf energy or petrochemical feedstocks should expect near-term price pressure and sourcing disruptions. The rescinded Hormuz toll proposal suggests the administration is willing to adjust positions quickly under commercial pressure—but the blockade itself remains.

**For U.S. allies,** the European defense spending surge is real, but the EDA's efficiency finding is sobering. Money is flowing without proportional increases in readiness or interoperability. Baltic states and Finland are hedging against U.S. reliability under the current administration by accelerating bilateral European defense agreements. Australia, Japan, and South Korea are watching China's SLBM test and the U.S. Coast Guard redeployment for indications about how much American bandwidth remains for Indo-Pacific deterrence.

## What to Watch

**Iran negotiations.** The Washington Post reported on July 10 that the U.S. and Iran agreed to continue "talks" even as the ceasefire was declared over. The parameters of those talks remain undisclosed. Watch whether Oman returns as a back-channel intermediary, as it did during the initial ceasefire negotiations. If Iran's strikes on commercial vessels continue beyond mid-July without a diplomatic track reasserting itself, a broader naval engagement becomes more likely.

**Fedorov's political trajectory.** His post-dismissal public statements attacking the military old guard suggest he intends to remain a political force. The scale of the Kyiv protests indicates his drone-warfare program had created a constituency. Watch whether he forms or joins a political opposition movement, and how Zelensky responds if the protests persist.

**PLA exercises near Taiwan.** The SLBM test and Joint Sea-2026 exercise ran concurrently with the Iran ceasefire collapse—a possible coincidence, but also a possible coordination of timing. ISW noted no direct Taiwan Strait confrontation, but the pattern of overlapping military demonstrations during periods of U.S. operational distraction is well-established. Any PLA announcement of exercises near Taiwan or in the South China Sea in the coming two to four weeks would indicate a deliberate escalation window.

**European defense efficiency.** The EDA report's finding that spending increases haven't improved efficiency creates a political opening for critics of the rearmament program. European governments have two to three months to show procurement results before the next parliamentary review cycle. Watch German and Polish defense procurement announcements, as both countries are anchoring the European buildup numerically.
